## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Flock Safety operates a network of over 83,000 Automated License Plate Reader (ALPR) cameras across the United States. This network captures the license plate, location, date, and time of every vehicle that passes any camera — millions of data points per day nationwide. This data is stored, searchable, and shareable across jurisdictions without a warrant, without probable cause, and without individualized suspicion.

This memorandum concludes that **Flock Safety's ALPR network, as currently operated, violates the Fourth, First, and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution** and that municipalities have both the authority and the obligation to regulate or prohibit its use within their jurisdictions.

## I. THE FOURTH AMENDMENT — UNREASONABLE SEARCH

### A. The Applicable Doctrine

The Fourth Amendment provides: "The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause..."

A "search" occurs when the government (or a private entity acting at the government's behest) infringes upon a person's "reasonable expectation of privacy." *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967).

### B. *Carpenter v. United States* Controls

In *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), the Supreme Court held that the warrantless acquisition of 127 days of cell-site location information (CSLI) constituted a Fourth Amendment search. Chief Justice Roberts, writing for the Court, reasoned:

> "A person does not surrender all Fourth Amendment protection by venturing into the public sphere. To the contrary, what one seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public, may be constitutionally protected." (Slip Op. at 15, citing *Katz*, 389 U.S. at 351-52.)

The Court recognized that while a single trip down a public road is not protected, the **aggregate** of location data over time creates a "detailed, comprehensive picture of one's public movements" that reveals "familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations." This, the Court held, "is not simply a dragnet — it is a net that literally follows every citizen constantly." (Slip Op. at 18.)

### C. Application to Flock Safety

Flock Safety's ALPR network presents an even more compelling Fourth Amendment violation than the CSLI at issue in *Carpenter* for the following reasons:

| Factor | *Carpenter* (CSLI) | Flock Safety ALPR Network |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Duration | 127 days | Indefinite — data retained 7+ days but network operates continuously |
| Data points per person | ~140 per day (cell tower pings) | Every trip, every day — potentially hundreds per week |
| Geographic scope | Cell tower sector (approx. 1-20 sq miles per ping) | Exact GPS-grade location — *every camera is a precise coordinate* |
| Network size | Cellular provider's towers | 83,000+ cameras nationwide, single-query access |
| What it reveals | General location | **Exact location + time + vehicle + pattern of life** |
| Third-party doctrine | Applied to business records (limited) | Vendor claims 7-day retention but data flows to national shared network |

**Key distinction:** *Carpenter* involved a cellular provider turning over records in response to a court order (not even a warrant — a § 2703(d) order). Flock's architecture goes further: police and their vendors **actively search** the database without any judicial authorization, using criteria that can reconstruct weeks of a person's daily routes, identify who they associate with, and map their patterns of life.

### D. The Mosaic Theory

The "mosaic theory" — which *Carpenter* adopted — holds that "the whole may be greater than the sum of its parts." A single trip past a camera is not a search; the **aggregation** of every trip over time is.

Flock's "Convoy" feature (now banned in this model ordinance) actively identifies **associations** between vehicles traveling together — effectively mapping social networks and organizational affiliations without any suspicion of wrongdoing. This is precisely the kind of surveillance the Fourth Amendment was designed to prevent.

### E. The Third-Party Doctrine Does Not Apply

The government may argue that license plates are "knowingly exposed to the public" and therefore unprotected under the third-party doctrine (*Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979)). This argument fails for three reasons:

1. ***Carpenter* rejected the same logic.** The Court held that even though cell-site data is "shared" with a cellular provider, the *aggregate* track of a person's movements over time is not voluntarily exposed in any meaningful sense. The same applies to ALPR data: a person has no meaningful choice about whether their license plate is captured by every camera they pass.

2. **The third-party doctrine was developed for discrete, targeted records** — a single phone number dialed (*Smith*), bank records of a specific account (*Miller*). It was never designed for mass, suspicionless, continuous surveillance of an entire population.

3. **Flock's data-sharing architecture multiplies the harm.** A person may "knowingly expose" their license plate to a single camera, but they do not consent to having that data shared with every law enforcement agency in the country without a warrant.

## II. THE FIRST AMENDMENT — CHILLING PROTECTED ACTIVITY

### A. The Doctrine

The First Amendment protects not only the right to speak, but the right to **associate** with others, to **assemble** peaceably, and to be **free from government surveillance that chills** these activities. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449 (1958); *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 594 U.S. ___ (2021).

### B. Documented Harms

During the 2025 "No Kings" protests, nineteen (19) law enforcement agencies conducted dozens of searches of Flock's database specifically tied to protest activity. Surveillance of political protest — even of public activity — chills participation and violates the First Amendment. As the Supreme Court held in *NAACP v. Alabama*, compelled disclosure of associational ties "may induce members to withdraw from the Association and dissuade others from joining it because of fear of exposure of their beliefs shown through their associations."

Flock's "Convoy" feature — which identifies vehicles traveling together — is a direct mechanism for mapping associational networks without any suspicion of criminal activity.

### C. Additional First Amendment Targets

EFF investigations documented that Delaware State Police used Flock to search for data related to animal rights activism (Direct Action Everywhere activities). The chilling effect is clear: **when people know the government is tracking who drives to a protest or an advocacy organization's meeting, fewer people attend.** This is precisely the kind of indirect suppression the First Amendment prohibits.

## III. THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT — EQUAL PROTECTION

### A. The Doctrine

The Equal Protection Clause prohibits government action that discriminates on the basis of race, ethnicity, or other protected characteristics, or that has a discriminatory impact that is not justified by a legitimate governmental interest. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976).

### B. Documented Harms

EFF investigations revealed that between June 2024 and October 2025, police performed hundreds of searches of Flock's database using racial and ethnic slurs as search terms — including searches targeting Romani communities. The Grand Prairie Police Department in Texas searched using the term "roma" six times while using Flock's "Convoy" feature to identify vehicles traveling together — effectively targeting an entire ethnic group without specifying any crime.

This is state-sponsored racial profiling, facilitated and enabled by a technology platform that makes mass, suspicionless searches trivially easy.

### C. Disparate Impact

Even when individual searches are not explicitly discriminatory, the **widespread deployment of ALPR cameras in minority neighborhoods** (a pattern documented in multiple cities) creates a disparate impact: minority residents are disproportionately surveilled, disproportionately searched, and disproportionately subject to the chilling effects described above.

## IV. STATE CONSTITUTIONAL PROTECTIONS

Even if federal Fourth Amendment law were deemed inapplicable (which it should not be), many state constitutions provide **broader** privacy protections than the federal Constitution:

| State | Provision | Strength |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **California** | Art. I, § 13 | Broader than 4A — explicit privacy right in Art. I, § 1 |
| **Washington** | Art. I, § 7 | "No person shall be disturbed in their private affairs" — broader than federal standard |
| **Illinois** | Art. I, § 6 | Rejects the third-party doctrine for some categories of data |
| **Hawaii** | Art. I, §§ 6-7 | Independent state privacy right, more protective |
| **Massachusetts** | Art. XIV | More protective warrant requirements under state common law |
| **Montana** | Art. II, § 10 | "Right of individual privacy is essential" — explicit, independent |
| **Alaska** | Art. I, § 22 | Explicit right to privacy |

*See generally State v. Jackson*, 76 P.3d 217 (Wash. 2003) (state constitution provides greater protection than the Fourth Amendment for location data); *People v. Johnson*, 935 N.E.2d 1103 (Ill. App. 2010) (GPS tracking violates state constitution despite *United States v. Jones* concurrence).

Congress should enact legislation that:
1. Requires a warrant based on probable cause for any search of ALPR data
2. Establishes a federal maximum data retention period of 7 days
3. Prohibits out-of-state and federal data sharing absent a warrant from the receiving jurisdiction
4. Creates a private right of action with statutory damages
5. Establishes an annual public audit requirement
6. Preempts weaker state laws while preserving states' ability to enact stronger protections

## VII. MUNICIPAL AUTHORITY TO ACT

Cities are not powerless. Municipalities possess broad police power to regulate surveillance technology within their borders, subject only to state preemption and federal constitutional limits. The model ordinance accompanying this memorandum is drafted to:

1. **Regulate city employees and city contracts** — express authority under municipal home rule
2. **Require warrants** — consistent with *Carpenter* and state constitutions
3. **Prohibit data sharing** — subject only to valid legal process (warrant)
4. **Require audits** — inherent municipal authority over public records
5. **Create a private right of action** — within the scope of state enabling statutes

**Preemption analysis:** No federal statute preempts municipal ALPR regulation. The federal Driver's Privacy Protection Act (18 U.S.C. §§ 2721-2725) regulates DMV records, not ALPR data. State preemption varies — California, Washington, and others expressly authorize local regulation of surveillance technology. A city attorney should review the state preemption landscape, but the trend is toward allowing — not restricting — local action.
